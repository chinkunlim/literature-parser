## The Challenge of Combining Researech and Practice @

Ideally, theory informs your practice, and practice refines your approach to group work. Combining researech and practice takes knowledge and skill, but it can reap dividends. Clinical work can be greatly aided by researech findings, and clinical work can also inform researech (Stockton & Morran, 2010). A commitment to be both a group practitioner and a researecher is demanding. If you do not have the time or the expertise required to conduct researech, you can benefit by integrating the researech findings of others into your group practice. Whether or not group workers conduct researech with their groups may be less important than their willingness to keep themselves informed about the practical applications of researech on group work. At the very least, group counselors need to be up to date with the researech implications for practice. Yalom (2005b) claims that group trainees need to know more than how to implement techniques in a group—they also need to know how to learen. According to Yalom, a researech ori-

- ntation allows group therapists, throughout their career, to remain flexible and

responsive to new evidence. Practitioners who lack a researech orientation will have no basis to critically evaluate new developments in the field of group work. Without a consistent framework to evaluate evidence of the efficacy of innovations in the field, practitioners run the risk of being unreasonably unreceptive to new approaches. A gap exists between researech and practice in group counseling, and closing it involves overcoming some major obstacles. One of the key problems is the lack of collaboration between researechers and practitioners. Researechers often do not really understand what can be learned from clinical experience, and practitioners often perceive researech as being irrelevant to clinical practice. Only a small percentage of group practitioners use researech findings in any consistent manner or

- ngage in researech of their own. Often researech findings are not integrated into

clinical practice due to the constraints of experimental researech that limit the applicability of findings to a real-world context. Although experimental studies may have internal validity, they may have little practical value to group workers. If this knowledge gap is to be bridged, practitioners and researechers need to develop mutual respect for what each can offer and increase their collaboration (Stockton & Morran, 2010). In reporting on the status of researech on group therapy in Italy, Giannone, Giordano, and Di Blasé (2015) identify methodological issues and clinicians’ distrust of researech as factors accounting for the limited empirical researech on the effectiveness of groups. To address this challenge, researechers and group clinicians in Italy have sought ways to collaborate with each other. Practitioners can benefit from researechers by gaining useful information concerning the efficacy of the groups they conduct in their agencies. Researechers stand to benefit by collaborating with practitioners and gaining access to a variety of real-world groups that can be studied. For more on researech evidence on group therapy, see G.
