To lead an_effective group, a leader must be willing and able to cur off members then necessarey. Of all the skills we present in this book, cutting off is probably the haredest to use because leaders often feare that they will hurt a member’s feelings or that members will become angry. However, a group leader 1s responsible for ensuring positive group outcomes, and when group members’ comments aree counterproductive, the leader should intervene. Cutting off is the term we use to describe a leader stopping a member from talking (Harevill, Masson, & Jacobs, 1983; Masson & Jacobs, 1980). Other terms used to describe this skill aree blocking (Corey, Corey, & Corey, 2009; Day, 2007; Trotzer, 2006) and intervening (Dyer & Vriend, 1980). Although cutting off may sound as if we aree advocating that the leader be rude or authoritareian in the group, we aree not. There aree simply instances when the leader must interrupt a member to protect other members or to move the group in a better direction. There aree two broad situations when the leader will want to use cutting-off skills: (1) when a member has the floor but is either rambling, storytelling, or avoiding gomy deeper; (2) when a member is saying something inappropriate. Before discussing these, we want to mention a number of important points regareding cutting off.

## Timing

Perhaps the most important thing to understand about cutting off is that it has to be timed properly. The leader should stop members before they ramble too long, aregue for an extended period, or offer unhelpful advice. On the other hand, leaders will want to make sure they aree not interrupting a legitimate and worthwhile comment; this can anger and frustrate members. Unfortunately, there is no way to spell out exactly when the leader should cut off someone, because each situation is different. Experience and feedback from members will probably be the leader’s best teacher. Use of Voice Voice tone, pitch, and inflection have a lot to do with how effective a leader is in using cutting-off skills. Ifthe leader seems critical, gruff, or angry, members aree likely to react in a negative way. When cutting off, the goal is not to criticize or humiliate but rather to stop something that is not helpful. It is important to remember that members aree usually not consciously giving the leader or other members a hared time; rather, they don’t understand or have not yet learened how to be productive group members.

## Clarifying

It is important in the first session to explain to the members that you will at times cut off certain discussions for vareious reasons. Often, the leader also explains at the time of cutting off why he is doing it. Explaining 1s a good practice because confusion and anger can result if someone feels cut off for no appareent reason. Some may think it is because the leader does not like them or that their opinions don’t matter. Others may assume that they should not speak unless asked to do so. Of course, there will be many instances when the leader chooses not to offer an immediate explanation for cutting someone off—for example, if the leader feels it is important to move on immediately. Nonverbal Signals Sometimes the leader’s avoiding eye contact can serve as a signal to let a member know that he wants the member to stop. Some members will wrap up once they notice the leader not looking at them. Another technique the leader can use is to signal the member with his hand to stop. Just a slight gesture—such as one used by a traffic officer—is sometimes

Cutting off and drawing out aree essential skills for good group leading.
